# Fayoum, Meidum & Hawara: The Pyramids Nobody Queues For

*A shared day journey with Sawa Tours*

Everyone who comes to Egypt sees the pyramids of Giza. Almost nobody drives ninety minutes further south, and that is exactly why this day exists. On the desert edge of the Fayoum depression stand two pyramids with stranger stories than their famous cousins: **Meidum**, the pyramid that collapsed — Egypt's first attempt at a true, smooth-sided pyramid, its casing long since fallen into the great mound of rubble that rings its exposed core — and **Hawara**, the pyramid of Amenemhat III, whose vast mortuary temple was the original **Labyrinth**: the building Herodotus visited and judged more astonishing than the pyramids themselves, and which has since vanished almost without trace.

Between and beyond them lies the **Fayoum** itself — the great green basin that drew the pharaohs of the Middle Kingdom out of the Nile valley, watered by an ancient channel of the river and gathered around a lake that was old when Egypt was young. After a morning among the pyramids, the afternoon belongs to the oasis: lunch in the pottery village of **Tunis**, and the shore of **Lake Qarun**, the remnant of the lake the ancients called Moeris.

This is a small-group shared departure, which means the pleasure of travelling alongside like-minded explorers, the ease of fully organised service, a knowledgeable guide, and a price that opens the oasis to everyone. *Sawa* means "together," and that is the spirit of the day — good company, quiet sites, and roads most visitors never take. Your seat is confirmed the moment you book, and the journey runs as a set group departure.

## Good to Know Before You Go

**Wear comfortable shoes.** The pyramid sites are desert ground — sand, rubble and uneven paths — so sturdy footwear matters more here than at the manicured sites.

**Bring sun protection.** Almost the whole day is open to the sky, so sunscreen, sunglasses and a hat make the desert hours a pleasure rather than a test.

**Carry a reusable water bottle.** It's a long day — eleven hours door to door — and staying hydrated keeps you going from site to site.

**Dress in layers.** The desert can be cool in the early morning and hot by midday; layers you can shed and reclaim serve the day best.

**Have your camera or phone ready.** The stepped core of Meidum rising from its rubble, the lake at golden hour, and the pottery of Tunis are all worth carrying home.

**Bring small cash.** Small denominations are handy for tips, lunch, and anything the potters of Tunis tempt you with.

## Meidum — The Pyramid That Collapsed

The first site of the day is the strangest pyramid in Egypt, and the most instructive. Meidum was begun as a step pyramid and then converted — the first attempt anywhere to build a true, smooth-sided pyramid, at the very moment Egypt's builders were inventing the form, most likely under Sneferu, father of the builder of the Great Pyramid.

The attempt failed. The outer casing and packing collapsed into the enormous mound of debris that still rings the site, leaving the inner core standing alone like a vast tower on a hill of its own ruin. What remains is arguably more dramatic than an intact pyramid: a cross-section of how these buildings were actually made, standing in open desert with hardly another visitor in sight.

The payoff is the lesson written in rubble. Between Meidum's failure and Giza's perfection lie only a few decades — and standing here, you can see exactly what the builders learned.

## Hawara — The Vanished Labyrinth

Hawara looks, at first, like the day's quietest site: a weathered mudbrick pyramid, its stone casing long stripped away. It belongs to Amenemhat III, the great pharaoh of the Middle Kingdom's golden age — the dynasty that turned the Fayoum from marshland into Egypt's garden.

What makes the ground extraordinary is what stood beside the pyramid. Amenemhat's mortuary temple grew into a complex so vast and intricate that the Greeks called it the Labyrinth; Herodotus walked its courts and wrote that it surpassed even the pyramids, twelve covered courts and three thousand rooms, half of them underground. Almost nothing of it survives above the sand — quarried away over two thousand years — and part of the wonder of Hawara is standing on the site of a building that astonished the ancient world and simply vanished.

The payoff is the reversal. At Giza the monument survives and the story is famous; at Hawara the monument is gone and the story is the treasure.

## The Fayoum — The Oasis That Tempted Pharaohs

The Fayoum is not a true desert oasis but something rarer: a great green basin fed by an ancient arm of the Nile, gathered around the lake the ancients called Moeris. The Middle Kingdom pharaohs loved it — they drained its marshes, farmed its edges, hunted on its water, and raised their pyramids on its rim, the only dynasty to be drawn out of the Nile valley itself.

The afternoon gives you the oasis at its best. Tunis Village, on its hillside above the lake, has grown from a single Swiss potter's school in the 1960s into a whole village of ceramic studios, its lanes full of kilns and painted bowls. Below it stretches Lake Qarun, shallow, silvery and busy with birds — the shrunken heir of Moeris, still catching the late light the way it has for five thousand years.

The payoff is the change of register. After a morning of desert and ruin, the day ends green — lunch above the palms, potters at their wheels, and the oldest lake in Egypt going gold as you turn for home.
