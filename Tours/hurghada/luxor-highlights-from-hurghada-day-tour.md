# Luxor Highlights from Hurghada: Karnak, Luxor Temple & the Valley of the Kings

*A shared day journey with Sawa Tours*

From a sunbed on the Red Sea, Luxor can feel like a rumour — the greatest open-air museum on earth, somewhere beyond the mountains to the west. It is closer than it looks. Two hundred and ninety kilometres of desert road separate your hotel from the Nile, and this day crosses them before breakfast so that you can spend the heart of the day where ancient Thebes once stood, and be back in Hurghada by bedtime.

It is a long day, and we would rather say so plainly than pretend otherwise: roughly four hours of driving each way, bracketing about six hours among the temples and tombs. What those six hours hold is the reason people cross the desert. On the East Bank, the vast temple city of **Karnak** and the riverside **Luxor Temple**, where the living worshipped. On the West Bank, where the dead were laid to rest, the painted royal tombs of the **Valley of the Kings**, the terraced **Temple of Hatshepsut** against its cliff, and the two seated giants of the **Colossi of Memnon**.

This is a small-group shared departure — a mixed group of travellers from different hotels and different countries, one air-conditioned minivan, one licensed Egyptologist guide, and never more than eight of you. *Sawa* means "together," and a day this long is better in good company: the drive passes faster, the stories are shared, and the price comes down as the group grows. Your seat is held the moment you reserve, and nothing is charged until the date confirms.

## Good to Know Before You Go

**It starts before dawn.** Pickup is around 5:00 AM. Ask your hotel for a breakfast box the night before — most Red Sea resorts will prepare one — and bring it with you for the road.

**Wear comfortable shoes.** Karnak is enormous, the Valley of the Kings involves slopes and steps inside the tombs, and Hatshepsut's temple is approached by long ramps. Supportive footwear makes the difference between a good day and a sore one.

**Sun protection matters.** Hat, sunglasses and sunscreen — the West Bank in particular offers little shade, and even in winter the midday sun is strong.

**Bring cash for extras.** Three tombs are included in your Valley of the Kings ticket. The tombs of Tutankhamun and Seti I carry separate tickets sold on site, as does the tomb of Nefertari in the Valley of the Queens. Your guide will tell you honestly whether they are worth it for you.

**Carry your passport.** The road between the Red Sea and the Nile Valley passes police checkpoints, and your guide may need to show the group's documents.

**Dress light, but pack a layer.** Pre-dawn and the late-evening return can be cool from October to March, while the middle of the day is warm year-round.

# Day 1 — Across the Desert to Thebes

## The Shape of the Day

**05:00 AM — Pickup in Hurghada**
Your driver collects you from your hotel in the dark. You meet your fellow travellers as the minivan fills, settle in, and sleep or watch the dawn come up over the Eastern Desert.

**05:30 AM — The Desert Road**
South along the coast to Safaga, then west through the mountains and across the desert to the Nile. There is a comfort stop along the way. Arrival in Luxor is around 9:00 to 9:30 AM, depending on the checkpoints.

**09:30 AM — Karnak Temple**
Your Egyptologist leads you through the avenue of ram-headed sphinxes, the Great Hypostyle Hall of 134 columns, the obelisks of Hatshepsut and Tuthmosis, and the sacred lake. About an hour and a half on site.

**11:15 AM — Luxor Temple**
A short drive along the river to Karnak's elegant companion — the temple of Amenhotep III and Ramses II, with the restored Avenue of Sphinxes running from its gate back toward Karnak.

**12:15 PM — Lunch Stop (optional)**
A break at a local restaurant on the way to the West Bank for anyone who would like a proper meal. Lunch is paid directly; your guide will steer you to somewhere reliable.

**01:15 PM — Valley of the Kings**
Across the bridge to the West Bank and into the valley where the New Kingdom pharaohs were buried. Your ticket admits you to three of the tombs open that day; your guide briefs you outside, as guiding inside the tombs is not permitted.

**03:00 PM — Temple of Hatshepsut**
The terraced mortuary temple of Egypt's great female pharaoh, set into the amphitheatre of cliffs at Deir el-Bahari. About forty-five minutes.

**03:50 PM — Colossi of Memnon**
A short stop before the two seated statues of Amenhotep III, all that stands of what was once the largest temple in Thebes.

**04:15 PM — The Return Drive**
Back across the desert as the light goes golden and then dark. A comfort stop on the way.

**08:30 PM — Drop-off in Hurghada**
Arrival back at your hotel in the evening, in time for a late dinner.

## Karnak — The Temple That Kept Growing

Karnak is not one temple but a city of them, built, enlarged and rebuilt by some thirty pharaohs over two thousand years. Nothing prepares you for the scale: the precinct of Amun alone covers more ground than many ancient towns.

The approach is a corridor of ram-headed sphinxes, and then the pylons open onto the Great Hypostyle Hall — 134 sandstone columns, the tallest of them twenty-one metres high, so thick that a dozen people linking hands could not circle one. Your guide reads the walls for you: Seti I and Ramses II at war, the gods receiving offerings, the cartouches of kings who added a court here and a gateway there as if the temple were never meant to be finished.

What Karnak gives you, more than any single monument, is a sense of time. Standing beside the obelisk of Hatshepsut, you are looking at stone quarried in Aswan and raised here nearly three and a half thousand years ago — and the hall around it was already old when Rome was founded.

## Luxor Temple — Where the Festival Ended

Luxor Temple sits in the middle of the modern town, close to the river, and its mood is quite different from Karnak's. Where Karnak sprawls, Luxor is composed: a long axis of courts and colonnades built largely by Amenhotep III and finished by Ramses II, whose colossal seated statues and lone surviving obelisk guard the entrance.

Once a year the statues of the gods travelled here from Karnak for the Opet festival, carried along the three-kilometre Avenue of Sphinxes that has now been excavated and reopened in its entirety. The temple has never really stopped being sacred: a Roman shrine was painted inside it, a mosque was built on top of it, and the mosque is still in use today, perched above the ancient floor level.

Your guide walks you through the layers — pharaonic, Roman, Christian, Islamic — and the temple becomes a short history of Egypt itself.

## The Valley of the Kings — Painted Rooms Under the Rock

For five hundred years the pharaohs of the New Kingdom gave up the pyramid and chose instead to be buried in secret, in tombs cut deep into a dry valley behind the Theban cliffs. Sixty-odd tombs have been found there, and a handful are open on any given day.

Your ticket covers three of them. Inside, the corridors descend through chamber after chamber painted with the texts that were meant to guide the king through the underworld — the Book of Gates, the Amduat, the Litany of Ra — and the colours, protected from sunlight for three millennia, are startlingly fresh. Guides explain the tombs outside and then let you walk them at your own pace, which is as it should be: the silence is part of it.

Tutankhamun's small, famous tomb and the magnificent tomb of Seti I are separately ticketed. Neither is required to make the visit worthwhile, and your guide will give you a straight opinion on whether they are worth the extra for you.

## Hatshepsut and the Colossi — The Monuments of the West Bank

The mortuary temple of Hatshepsut at Deir el-Bahari is unlike anything else in Egypt: three broad terraces climbing by ramps into a bay of golden cliffs, as modern in its lines as it is ancient in its story. Hatshepsut ruled as pharaoh in her own right for two decades, sent a trading expedition to the land of Punt that is recorded in relief on these walls, and was then systematically erased from the monuments by her successor. The temple survived the erasure; the reliefs of Punt — the incense trees, the fat queen, the exotic fish — are among the most charming in Egypt.

On the way back to the river, the Colossi of Memnon rise out of the fields: two weathered seated statues of Amenhotep III, eighteen metres high, which once flanked the gate of the largest temple ever built in Thebes. The temple is gone; the Colossi have kept their post for thirty-four centuries. It is the right place to end — a last look at the scale of what was here before the long road home.

## Returning to Hurghada

The drive back is quieter than the drive out. The group has shared a day now, and the desert outside turns from gold to violet to black as the minivan heads east toward the coast.

You arrive at your hotel in the evening with a long day behind you and a good deal to think about — a city of temples, a valley of painted tombs, and the knowledge that all of it was waiting just across the mountains the whole time. You did it in one day, and you did it *sawa*, together.
