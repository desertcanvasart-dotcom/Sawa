# Dendera & Abydos from Hurghada: the Far Temples Day Tour

*A shared day journey with Sawa Tours*

Most visitors to Egypt never see Dendera or Abydos. They lie north of Luxor, off the usual cruise route, and the coaches rarely make the detour — which is exactly why the two best-preserved temples in the country are also two of the quietest. For a traveller based on the Red Sea the geography is kinder than it is for most: the desert road from Hurghada meets the Nile at Qena, and Dendera stands just across the river.

This day takes you there. First the **Temple of Hathor at Dendera**, almost intact to its roof, with a painted astronomical ceiling that has been cleaned back to its original blues and golds. Then north to **Abydos**, the holiest city of ancient Egypt, where the Temple of Seti I holds the finest raised reliefs ever carved and the famous King List that gave Egyptology its chronology. You will see both with a licensed Egyptologist, in a group that is never larger than eight, and you will very often have the halls to yourselves.

It is a full day on the road — around twelve hours door to door — but it is a day of two temples rather than six, with time to stand still in each. *Sawa* means "together," and the shared minivan is what makes a journey this far off the beaten path affordable: the more of you who join, the lower the price for everyone. Reserve a seat for free; nothing is charged until the date confirms.

## Good to Know Before You Go

**Early start.** Pickup is around 5:30 AM. Ask your hotel for a breakfast box the evening before.

**Bring a torch or use your phone light.** The crypts at Dendera and some of the chapels at Abydos are dim, and a small light reveals the detail of the reliefs.

**Comfortable shoes and sun protection.** There are stairs to the roof at Dendera and long stretches of open ground at Abydos. Hat, sunglasses and sunscreen all earn their place.

**Lunch is not included.** There is a lunch stop at a local restaurant between the two temples for anyone who would like one, paid directly. Bring snacks if you would rather keep moving.

**Carry your passport.** The road crosses police checkpoints between the Red Sea and the Nile Valley.

**Modest dress is appreciated.** These are working rural towns, and covered shoulders and knees are the comfortable choice for everyone.

## Dendera — The House of Hathor, Almost Whole

Dendera is the temple that shows you what the others once looked like. Built in the last centuries before Christ by the Ptolemies and finished under the Roman emperors, it was buried to its roof in sand and rubbish for most of the following two thousand years, and so it survived nearly complete — walls, ceilings, stairways, crypts, and the roof you can still walk on.

The first sight is the hypostyle hall, where twenty-four columns rise to capitals carved with the face of Hathor, goddess of love, music and joy, and where the ceiling has recently been cleaned of centuries of soot. The colour that emerged is astonishing: deep blue skies, the goddess Nut swallowing the sun each evening, the signs of the zodiac, the boats of the hours. Beyond lie the inner halls and the sanctuary, and beneath them the narrow crypts where the temple's treasures were kept, carved with the reliefs that the internet has made famous as the "Dendera light."

Climb the ceremonial staircase to the roof — the same route the priests took each New Year to carry Hathor's statue up to greet the sunrise — and you find the small chapel of Osiris and, in its ceiling, a copy of the Dendera zodiac. The original is in the Louvre; your guide will tell you how it got there.

## Abydos — The City of Osiris

Abydos was to ancient Egypt what Mecca or Jerusalem is to their faiths: the burial place of Osiris, lord of the dead, and the place every Egyptian hoped to visit once before dying — or at least to be carried past in a model boat after. The kings of the First Dynasty were buried here; three thousand years later pilgrims were still coming.

The temple that Seti I built here, and his son Ramses II finished, is the jewel of the site. Its reliefs are carved in raised relief rather than cut into the stone, a slower and more expensive technique, and the result is the most refined sculpture of the New Kingdom: the king offering to the gods in profile so delicate that the muscles of a forearm and the folds of linen are legible from across the room, much of the colour still clinging to the walls.

Along one corridor is the Abydos King List — seventy-six cartouches of Seti's predecessors, in order, from Menes to himself. It leaves out the heretic Akhenaten and the female pharaoh Hatshepsut, and in doing so it tells you as much about Seti's politics as about Egypt's past. It also gave nineteenth-century scholars the framework on which the whole chronology of ancient Egypt was built.

Behind the temple lies the Osireion, a massive granite structure sunk below ground level and often half-flooded, built in a style so archaic that its date is still debated. It is the kind of place that makes a day trip feel like an expedition.

## Returning to Hurghada

The drive back east is the quiet part of the day. The sugar-cane fields give way to the desert, the desert to the mountains, and the mountains, eventually, to the first glimpse of the sea.

You arrive at your hotel in the early evening having seen two temples that most people who visit Egypt never reach — in a small group, with a guide who knew them well, and for a price that only sharing the road makes possible. *Sawa*, together.
