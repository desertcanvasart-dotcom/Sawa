# Nubian Temples of Lake Nasser: Kalabsha, Beit el-Wali & Kertassi

*A shared half-day journey with Sawa Tours*

Just south of the High Dam, on a rocky island at the edge of Lake Nasser, stand three temples that almost nobody visits. They were not built here. When the dam was finished in the 1960s and the lake began to rise, the monuments of Lower Nubia were cut into blocks, carried out of the valley, and rebuilt on higher ground — Abu Simbel most famously, but also these: the great sandstone **Temple of Kalabsha**, the small painted rock-chapel of **Beit el-Wali**, and the graceful **Kiosk of Kertassi**, standing at the water's edge like a question mark.

Reaching them is part of the pleasure. A short drive from your Aswan hotel to the dock above the dam, a fifteen-minute crossing by motor boat with the lake opening around you, and then two and a half hours on an island you will very likely share with no one but your own group. The temples are complete, the carvings crisp, the setting — bare granite, blue water, open desert — as Nubian as anything in Egypt.

It is a half day, back at your hotel for lunch, and it slots easily beside a Nile cruise departure or an afternoon at Philae. *Sawa* means "together," and the boat and the guide are what the group shares: the more travellers who join the date, the lower the price per person. ## Kalabsha — Rome's Temple to a Nubian God

The Temple of Kalabsha is the largest and best preserved of the rescued monuments after Abu Simbel, and the surprise is who built it. Though it stands on a site sacred since the New Kingdom, the temple you walk through was raised under the Roman emperor Augustus, around 30 BC, in honour of Mandulis — a Nubian sun god whom the Egyptians had adopted and the Romans were happy to inherit.

The approach is a long stone causeway from the water, then a pylon, an open court, and a hypostyle hall whose column capitals are carved with lotus and palm. On the walls, Augustus and later emperors appear in the full regalia of pharaohs, offering to Mandulis, Isis and Osiris — a Roman emperor who never set foot in Egypt, drawn as the son of Ra. Inscriptions from later centuries are scattered through the temple: a decree in Greek expelling pigs from the precinct, a Meroitic king's victory text, Christian crosses from when the sanctuary became a church.

The whole building — some thirteen thousand blocks — was dismantled by a German team in 1962–63 and rebuilt here, forty kilometres from its original site. Your guide points out the seams.

## Beit el-Wali and Kertassi — The Small Temples

A short walk from Kalabsha, the rock-cut temple of Beit el-Wali is older and more intimate. Ramses II had it carved early in his reign, and the forecourt reliefs are a comic-strip of his campaigns: Nubians bringing tribute of leopards, giraffes and gold; the king in his chariot scattering Libyans and Syrians; princes leading prisoners. Inside, in the vestibule and sanctuary, the colour is remarkably preserved — reds, yellows and blues on scenes of the king before the gods of the cataract.

The Kiosk of Kertassi is smaller still: six columns and a lintel, rebuilt at the water's edge, two of them with the face of Hathor on their capitals. It once stood at the entrance to a sandstone quarry that supplied the temples of Nubia, and it is thought to have been dedicated to Isis. It is a slight building, but in its setting — granite, water, and the empty desert beyond — it makes the morning's point better than anything else: that a whole landscape of temples was lifted out of a drowning valley and set down again, stone by stone, so that you could stand here.
