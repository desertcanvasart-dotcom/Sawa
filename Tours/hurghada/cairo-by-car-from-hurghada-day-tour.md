# Cairo by Car from Hurghada: Pyramids, Grand Egyptian Museum & the Citadel

*A shared day journey with Sawa Tours*

There are travellers who come to the Red Sea for the diving and the sun, and who would still never forgive themselves for flying home without having seen the pyramids. This day exists for them. It is the longest day Sawa runs out of Hurghada — around nineteen hours door to door, more than ten of them on the road — and we will not dress that up. What it buys you is a full day in Cairo without a hotel night and without an airport: the **Giza Plateau** and the **Great Sphinx** in the morning, the new **Grand Egyptian Museum** at midday, and the **Citadel of Saladin** and a taste of **Khan el-Khalili** in the afternoon, before the long run back to the coast.

We do it by car rather than by coach, and the difference is not small. A shared minivan of at most eight travellers leaves when you are ready, moves through Cairo traffic as a car can, stops where a coach cannot, and gives your Egyptologist a conversation instead of a microphone. It also means the morning drive is a little faster and the day a little less tiring than the bus version you may have been offered.

*Sawa* means "together." Everyone in the minivan has made the same calculation — one very long day for the chance to stand under the Great Pyramid — and the price of making it comes down for all of you as the group grows. Reserve a seat for free; nothing is charged until the date confirms.

## Good to Know Before You Go

**It is a 4:00 AM pickup.** Order a breakfast box from your hotel the evening before. The first hours are in the dark, and most people sleep.

**Comfortable shoes above all.** The Giza Plateau is sandy and uneven, the Grand Egyptian Museum is vast, and the Citadel is a climb. You will walk a good deal.

**Sun protection.** The plateau has almost no shade. Hat, sunglasses, sunscreen and water for the morning.

**Lunch is not included.** There is a lunch stop at a local restaurant after the museum, paid directly. Your guide will suggest somewhere reliable and quick.

**Bring cash and small change.** For lunch, for the bazaar, for the optional camel ride at the panorama, and for the separately ticketed interior of the Great Pyramid if you want to go inside.

**Passport in your bag.** The Red Sea road has police checkpoints, and the museum may ask for identification at the gate.

**Expect to be back late.** Drop-off at your hotel is usually around 11:00 PM. Plan a lazy morning for the day after.

# Day 1 — The Long Road to the Pyramids

## The Shape of the Day

**04:00 AM — Pickup in Hurghada**
Your driver collects you from your hotel before dawn. The minivan gathers the rest of the group and heads north out of town.

**04:30 AM — The Red Sea Road**
North along the coast and then north-west across the Eastern Desert toward Cairo — about five hours, with a comfort stop around halfway. Sunrise comes somewhere over the desert.

**09:30 AM — The Giza Plateau**
Straight to the pyramids before the heat and the crowds. Your Egyptologist walks you through the three pyramids of Khufu, Khafre and Menkaure, the panorama viewpoint where all three line up, and the story of how and why they were built.

**11:00 AM — The Great Sphinx & the Valley Temple**
Down to the Sphinx and the granite temple beside it, where the body of Khafre was prepared for burial.

**11:45 AM — The Grand Egyptian Museum**
A short drive to the museum beside the plateau for a guided visit of about two and a half hours: the Grand Staircase, the main galleries, and the complete collection of Tutankhamun, displayed together for the first time.

**02:15 PM — Lunch Stop (optional)**
A proper meal at a local restaurant in Giza or central Cairo, paid directly.

**03:30 PM — The Citadel of Saladin**
Across the city to the medieval fortress on the Muqattam spur: the alabaster Mosque of Muhammad Ali and the terrace view over the whole of Cairo to the pyramids you stood beneath that morning.

**04:45 PM — Khan el-Khalili**
A short, guided walk into the old bazaar — long enough for a mint tea at El Fishawy and a look at the lanes of copper and lanterns, not long enough to get lost.

**05:30 PM — The Return Drive**
Out of Cairo in the evening rush and south toward the coast, with a comfort stop.

**11:00 PM — Drop-off in Hurghada**
Back at your hotel late in the evening.

*Cairo's traffic and the museum's entry flow are the two variables; your guide builds the afternoon around them.*

## The Giza Plateau — Standing Under the Only Wonder Left

Of the Seven Wonders of the ancient world, one is still standing, and you reach it at half past nine in the morning with the sun still low and the plateau still quiet. The Great Pyramid of Khufu was the tallest building on earth for nearly four thousand years. Up close it stops being a shape and becomes a mountain made by hand — more than two million blocks, each heavier than a car, laid with a precision that modern surveyors still find hard to explain.

Your guide takes you from the base of Khufu to the pyramid of Khafre, which keeps its cap of smooth casing stone, and the smaller pyramid of Menkaure, and then out to the panorama point in the desert where the three align and the camera finally has room for them. Along the way the myths are cleared away — no slaves, no aliens — and replaced with something better: the organised, well-fed, proud workforce whose own tombs and bakeries have been excavated beside the plateau.

Then down to the Sphinx. Seventy-three metres long and carved from a single outcrop of limestone, it has watched the sunrise from the foot of Khafre's causeway for four and a half thousand years, and it is smaller, stranger and more moving in person than any photograph suggests.

## The Grand Egyptian Museum — Tutankhamun Comes Home

For a century, Egypt's greatest treasures were squeezed into a handsome but hopelessly overcrowded museum in central Cairo. The Grand Egyptian Museum was built to end that. It stands within sight of the pyramids, a vast wedge of stone and glass, and it opened in full at the end of 2025 as the largest archaeological museum in the world.

The visit begins under the eleven-metre colossus of Ramses II in the atrium and climbs the Grand Staircase, lined with statues of kings and gods, toward a window framing the pyramids themselves. Beyond lie the main galleries, arranged by period and theme, and at the heart of the building the complete tomb treasure of Tutankhamun — more than five thousand objects, including the golden mask, the nested shrines and the chariots, together in one place for the first time since they were taken out of the tomb in the 1920s.

A museum this size could swallow a week. Your Egyptologist keeps the visit to two and a half focused hours, choosing the galleries that matter and telling the stories that connect the objects to the plateau you have just walked.

## The Citadel and Khan el-Khalili — Medieval Cairo in an Afternoon

From the pharaohs to the sultans. The Citadel was begun by Saladin in 1176 to defend Cairo from the Crusaders and remained the seat of Egypt's rulers for seven hundred years. Its crown is the Mosque of Muhammad Ali, built in the 1830s in the Ottoman style with its clustered domes and pencil minarets, its walls clad in alabaster, and from its terrace the whole of Cairo lies below you — the minarets of the old city in the foreground, the Nile beyond, and on a clear afternoon, the pyramids on the horizon.

Then down into the old city for a short walk through Khan el-Khalili, the bazaar that has traded on the same lanes since the fourteenth century. Your guide leads you through the spice and copper alleys to El Fishawy, the café that claims not to have closed in two hundred years, for a glass of mint tea before the road. It is a taste rather than a meal — but it is the right note on which to leave Cairo: alive, loud, and still trading.

## Returning to Hurghada

The last stretch is the longest, and most of the group sleeps through it. Cairo's lights thin out behind you, the desert closes in, and somewhere after midnight-dark the first hotel signs of the coast appear.

You arrive back late, tired, and with the one thing you came for: you have stood under the Great Pyramid, looked the Sphinx in the eye, and seen the mask of Tutankhamun in its new home — all in a single day from the Red Sea, in a small group that shared the road. *Sawa*, together.
