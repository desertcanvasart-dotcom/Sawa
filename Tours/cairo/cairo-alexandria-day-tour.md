# Cairo to Alexandria: The Mediterranean Face of Ancient Egypt

*A shared day journey with Sawa Tours*

Almost everyone comes to Egypt for the same things — pharaohs, pyramids, the desert — and so Alexandria tends to get reduced to a checklist, a few sights ticked off on the coast. We see it as the other half of the story. This is where Egypt stops facing the desert and turns to face the sea: a Greek and Roman city, founded by Alexander the Great, that was once the great cosmopolitan capital of the ancient Mediterranean. Spend a day here and you meet an Egypt of scholars, sailors, and mixed bloodlines — where even the tombs are carved half in Egyptian style and half in Roman.

The day moves through the layers of that mixed city. The **Catacombs of Kom El Shuqafa**, the largest Greco-Roman necropolis in Egypt, where two civilisations meet underground. **Pompey's Pillar**, a single towering granite column standing for a whole vanished temple. The **National Museum of Alexandria**, where the city's long story is gathered under one roof. And the **Roman Amphitheatre**, the only one of its kind in Egypt.

This is a small-group shared departure, which means the pleasure of travelling alongside like-minded explorers, the ease of fully organised service, and a price that opens the Mediterranean coast to everyone. *Sawa* means "together," and that is the spirit of the day — good company, a knowledgeable guide, and a comfortable seat for the road there and back. Your seat is confirmed the moment you book, and the journey runs as a set group departure.

## Good to Know Before You Go

**Wear comfortable shoes.** The day involves a good deal of walking and standing across historical sites, including underground stairs at the catacombs, so footwear you can move easily in matters.

**Dress modestly where it's asked of you.** At religious and cultural sites, covering shoulders and knees keeps you respectful of the place and comfortable moving between them.

**Bring sun protection.** Several stops are open to the sky, so sunscreen, sunglasses, and a hat make the outdoor hours a pleasure rather than a test.

**Check before you photograph.** Photography is welcome at most stops, but a few places — the National Museum and parts of the catacombs among them — have their own rules, so it's worth a quick check before you raise the camera.

**Carry a reusable water bottle.** It's a long day with three hours of road at either end, and staying hydrated keeps you going from site to site.

## The Catacombs of Kom El Shuqafa — Two Worlds Underground

The day's first stop sets its whole theme. You descend a spiral stair into the cool dark beneath the city, into the largest Greco-Roman necropolis in Egypt — a sprawling, multi-level burial place cut deep into the rock.

What makes it singular is written all over the walls. This is where the city's two cultures meet most vividly: ancient Egyptian gods and funerary traditions carved side by side with Greek and Roman style, sometimes blended into single strange figures — Egyptian deities in Roman dress, classical robes on Egyptian forms. Nowhere captures Alexandria's mixed soul more directly than down here.

The payoff is that you understand the city before you've even seen it in daylight. Everything you visit afterward is a variation on this one idea — two worlds layered into one — and you've already met it underground, carved into the tombs.

---

## Pompey's Pillar — One Column for a Lost World

Back above ground, a short drive brings you to Pompey's Pillar, a single column of polished red granite standing some twenty-eight metres tall above the surrounding city, with two sphinxes keeping watch at its base.

The pillar is something of a beautiful misunderstanding — it has nothing to do with Pompey at all, and was raised in the late Roman period. What matters is what it's the last of: it once stood within the Serapeum, a grand temple complex that was among the most important religious and intellectual centres of the ancient city. Almost all of that is gone now.

And that is the quiet power of the place. A whole complex of temple and learning, reduced to one upright column on a low hill — a single survivor standing in for everything around it that time took away.

---

## The National Museum of Alexandria — The City Under One Roof

From the open sites you move indoors, to a museum housed in an elegant old mansion that once belonged to the city's wealthy, its galleries calm and ordered after the morning's heat.

What the museum gives you is the thread. Its collection runs across the full sweep of Alexandria's history — Pharaonic, Greco-Roman, Coptic, and Islamic — gathered floor by floor in one place. After a morning of scattered ruins, here the city's story is laid out whole, each era following the last.

The payoff is clarity. The catacombs and the pillar suddenly fit into a sequence; you leave understanding not just what you saw, but where each piece sits in the long life of a city that has reinvented itself again and again.

---

## The Roman Amphitheatre — Where the City Gathered

The final stop brings you to the Roman Amphitheatre, and it's a fitting place to end — because where the day began among the dead, it closes among the living.

This is the only Roman theatre in Egypt, a graceful horseshoe of marble seating laid out in tiers around a central stage, part of a larger Roman quarter unearthed in the heart of the modern city. The thirteen rows of marble seats held a real audience, in a city that prized performance, debate, and public spectacle.

Sit on the cool stone for a moment and the abstraction falls away. Alexandrians came here to be entertained, to gather, to be a public — and that simple human fact, after a day of tombs and temples, is what stays with you.

## Returning to Cairo

The highway carries you back south through the delta, and the shift is one you feel as much as see: from a city that lives toward the open sea back to the capital pressed against the edge of the desert.

Cairo gathers you in again with all its noise and motion — the medieval city and the modern one tangled together along the Nile, a world away in mood from the breezy Mediterranean coast you spent the day on.

The tour ends here, in the late afternoon. What you've really done is meet the other Egypt — Greek, Roman, and seafaring, layered culture upon culture — and you met it the best way there is, in good company, sharing each discovery as it came, *sawa*, together.
