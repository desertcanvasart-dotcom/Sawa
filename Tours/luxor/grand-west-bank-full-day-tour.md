# The Grand West Bank: From the Pharaohs' Tombs to the Village That Built Them

*A shared day journey with Sawa Tours*

The West Bank of Luxor is usually sold as one thing: the place with the pharaohs' tombs. Visit the Valley of the Kings, tick the box, move on. But that misses what the West Bank really is. This was an entire society in death — not just kings, but their queens and children, their temples to the gods and the dead, and, most remarkably of all, the craftsmen who cut and painted the royal tombs with their own hands, who lived in their own village and were buried in their own small, exquisite tombs just over the hill. A full day here isn't about royalty. It's about a whole civilisation's relationship with death, top to bottom.

The day works its way through that world. The **Valley of the Kings**, where the New Kingdom pharaohs were hidden away; the **Valley of the Queens**, with the famous painted tomb of Nefertari; the towering **Colossi of Memnon**; the cliff-set **Temple of Hatshepsut**; the vast, vividly preserved **Temple of Medinet Habu**; and finally **Deir El Medina**, the village of the very artisans who made the whole necropolis possible.

This is a small-group shared departure, which means the pleasure of travelling alongside like-minded explorers, the ease of fully organised service, the depth a knowledgeable local guide brings, and a price that opens the whole West Bank to everyone. *Sawa* means "together," and a day this rich is far better shared — the small details pointed out, the connections drawn, the wonder doubled by good company. Your seat is confirmed the moment you book, and the journey runs as a set group departure.

## Good to Know Before You Go

**Wear comfortable shoes.** You'll climb in and out of tombs and cross wide temple grounds and uneven ground all day — footwear you can walk in for hours matters more than anything.

**Bring sunscreen and a hat.** Much of the West Bank is open desert with little shade, so real sun protection turns the bright hours into a pleasure rather than a test.

**Carry a reusable water bottle.** A full day in the dry heat asks a lot of you, and staying hydrated is what keeps you going from tomb to temple.

**Have your camera or phone ready.** The West Bank is endlessly photogenic — though photography is restricted inside some of the tombs, so your guide will let you know where it's allowed.

**Dress light and breathable.** Lightweight, natural fabrics keep you cool as you move between the open sites through the warmest part of the day.

## Valley of the Kings — The Pharaohs in Hiding

Your day begins in the most famous burial ground on earth, and the first thing to understand is that everything here was built to disappear.

Tired of having their pyramids robbed, the pharaohs of the New Kingdom hid their tombs instead — cut deep into the rock of a remote desert valley, sealed, and meant to be forgotten. With access to three of them, you descend into a world of colour: walls and ceilings covered in painted scenes and texts meant to carry the king safely through the afterlife, including the tomb of the boy king Tutankhamun, whose nearly intact burial astonished the world.

What stays with you is the strangeness of standing inside art that was never meant to be seen again. These were not monuments for the living; they were doorways to the next world, sealed shut — and you're walking through them.

---

## Valley of the Queens — The Royal Family at Rest

A short drive south brings you to the Valley of the Queens, where the wives and children of the pharaohs were laid to rest.

The valley's crowning glory is the tomb of Nefertari, beloved queen of Ramses II — widely held to be the most beautiful painted tomb in all of Egypt. Its walls glow with images of the queen moving among the gods, the colour so fresh and the craftsmanship so fine that it feels less like a tomb than a painted chapel.

The payoff is the shift in scale. After the grandeur of the kings, this is royal Egypt at a more human register — the family behind the throne, remembered with a tenderness and artistry that lingers.

---

## The Colossi of Memnon — Two Giants on the Plain

Out where the desert meets the green fields, two enormous seated figures rise from the ground — the Colossi of Memnon, each carved from a single block of stone and weathered by more than three thousand years.

They once guarded the entrance to the mortuary temple of Amenhotep III, the largest ever built on the West Bank, almost all of which has long since vanished. In Roman times one of the statues became famous for a strange sound it gave off at dawn, drawing curious travellers from across the ancient world to hear it "sing."

The stop is brief, but the impression isn't: two silent giants keeping watch over a temple that no longer exists.

---

## Temple of Hatshepsut — A Queen Carved Into the Cliff

Next rises one of the most striking buildings in Egypt — the mortuary temple of Hatshepsut, climbing in great stone terraces straight out of the foot of the cliffs at Deir el-Bahari.

Hatshepsut was among the very few women ever to rule as pharaoh in her own right, and she did so with rare success. Her temple matched her ambition: clean ranks of colonnaded terraces, chapels, and statues, decorated with scenes of her divine birth and her famous trading expedition to the distant land of Punt. Later rulers tried to erase her name from history — yet her temple still stands as one of the boldest on the bank.

It is architecture as statement, and standing before it you feel exactly what she intended: a ruler insisting, in stone, on her own greatness.

---

## Temple of Medinet Habu — The Best-Kept Secret of the Bank

The afternoon brings you to Medinet Habu, and for many travellers it quietly steals the day.

This was the mortuary temple of Ramesses III, and it is enormous — but what sets it apart is how much survives. Where so many temples have faded to bare stone, here the carvings are deep, crisp, and still carry their original colour in places, covering the walls with vivid scenes of battle, ritual, and royal power. The complex was also fortified, serving as a stronghold and a centre of administration, with a great gateway modelled on a foreign fortress.

The payoff is in the preservation. Walking through it, you get the rarest thing of all on the West Bank — a sense of how blazingly colourful and alive these temples once were, before time bleached them down to stone.

---

## Deir El Medina — The Village That Built the Valley

The day ends somewhere most tours never reach, and it's the perfect place to finish — Deir El Medina, the village of the workers who built and decorated the royal tombs.

This is where the painters, sculptors, and stonecutters of the Valley of the Kings actually lived, generation after generation, in a planned little town tucked into the hills. Their own tombs, though small, are some of the most charming on the whole bank — decorated by the very hands that worked on the kings', and often more intimate and lively for it. The village has even given up the everyday records of its people: their wages, their disputes, their letters, even the account of one of the first recorded labour strikes in history.

It is the most human stop of the day, and a deliberate one. After hours among gods and god-kings, you end with the real people — the craftsmen whose skill made all the splendour possible, and whose ordinary lives are written into the very hill they're buried in.

## The Day's End in Luxor

The drive back across the river returns you to the city in the late afternoon, the full sweep of the day behind you.

Luxor softens in the evening — the Nile gold with the lowering sun, feluccas drifting on the water, the great East Bank temples beginning to glow under their lights across the river from the necropolis you've just spent the day in. The living city and the city of the dead, face to face across the water.

The tour ends here. What you've really done is meet the entire society of the ancient West Bank — kings, queens, gods, and the working people who built it all — not as a checklist of tombs, but as a whole world, discovered in good company, *sawa*, together.
